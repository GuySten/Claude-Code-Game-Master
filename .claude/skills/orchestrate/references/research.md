# Research: what music psychology says (a digest)

A paraphrased digest, with each finding cited by chapter, of five chapters of Juslin & Sloboda (eds.), *Handbook of Music and Emotion: Theory, Research, Applications* (Oxford University Press, 2010): ch13 (Simonton, composition), ch14 (Gabrielsson & Lindström, structure and emotion), ch17 (Juslin, performance), ch21 (Huron & Margulis, expectancy and thrills), ch31 (Cohen, film). Its tables were not available, and nothing in it is about villains or madness directly: rules marked *(inference)* combine nearby findings. The device library (devices.md) cites it; the host's verdicts (table-feedback.md) outrank it.

## 1. Feature -> expression map

Strength key:
- **[strong]**: replicated across many studies, or called the clearest result by the authors.
- **[moderate]**: several studies agree, but it depends on context.
- **[weak]**: one study, a contested result, or an effect the authors call ambiguous.

### Tempo / note density (ch14 §14.3.1; ch17 §17.2.3, 17.2.5)
- **Overall:** tempo is generally rated the single most important factor [strong]. Studies cited for this include Gundlach 1935, Hevner 1937, Juslin 1997, Gagnon & Peretz 2003, Rigg 1964 and Scherer & Oshinsky 1977.
- **Fast tempo** (ch14) goes with:
  - activity and excitement, happiness and pleasantness
  - potency, surprise, flippancy or whimsy
  - **anger, uneasiness and fear**
- **Slow tempo** (ch14) goes with:
  - calm and peace, **sadness**
  - **dignity/solemnity**, **tenderness**
  - longing, boredom and disgust
- **Mainly an arousal cue:** tempo mostly moves the activation (arousal) axis; fast or slow tempo can carry either positive or negative valence (ch14) [strong]. Other cues can override it. In Rigg 1940b, a minor-mode phrase built on descending seconds still sounded sad at 160 bpm (ch14).
- **Slower is more serious:** in Behne 1972, slowing recorded pieces made them sound more serious and more complaining (ch14).
- **Tempo outweighs mode for happy versus sad:** Gagnon & Peretz 2003 found this (ch14). Children also become sensitive to tempo earlier than to mode (Dalla Bella et al. 2001, ch14).
- **Note density:** the number of notes per second also shapes how fast music feels. Density is higher when music expresses happiness, anger or fear than when it expresses sadness (ch14).
  - Fast tempo plus dense notes gives even higher activation.
  - Slow tempo plus sparse notes gives even lower activation.
  - Slow tempo with dense notes is ambiguous (Gabrielsson 1986/88, ch14).
- **Performance code** (ch17): sadness is played slowly, happiness quickly. Tempo, sound level and timbre have the largest effect on listeners' judgements of performances.

### Mode / key (ch14 §14.3.2; ch13)
- **Valence cue:** major goes with happy and minor with sad [strong for adults and children over about 6–8 years]. Mode mainly moves **valence** (ch14).
  - Major can also read as graceful, serene or **solemn**.
  - Minor can also read as dreamy, **dignified**, **tense**, disgusted or **angry** (ch14).
- **Mode is not decisive on its own.** A fast minor piece can sound happy (Bach's *Badinerie*), and loud or high chords sound happier whatever their mode (Heinlein 1928; ch14).
- **Mode and valence:** in Costa et al. 2004, mode was the only significant predictor of valence in 51 melodies (ch14).
- **Phrygian versus Ionian:** adults and children judged Phrygian settings more fitting for **condemnation/punishment** and Ionian (major) more fitting for salvation/reward (Hill, Kamenetsky & Trehub 1996; ch14) [single study].
- **Key characters:** there is no empirical support for particular keys carrying particular moods. In Powell & Dibben 2005, transposing a piece by a semitone changed nothing (ch14).
- **Minor and arousal** (ch13, Simonton): minor themes score higher on melodic originality because their transitions are rarer. Listeners tend to find minor themes more exotic or strange, and composers often pick minor for emotionally intense works. Simonton cites Meyer's view that minor goes with intense feeling and especially with sadness, suffering and anguish.

### Loudness / dynamics (ch14 §14.3.3; ch17)
- **Loud** goes with intensity/**power**, excitement, **tension**, **anger** and joy. In short: high activation and **potency** (ch14) [strong].
- **Soft** goes with softness, peace, **tenderness**, **sadness**, **solemnity** and also **fear**. In short: low activation, perhaps submissiveness (ch14).
- **Effect of loudness on arousal and valence** (Ilie & Thompson 2006, ch14):
  - Higher loudness raised both energy arousal and **tension arousal**.
  - Lower loudness raised valence.
- **Juslin 1997** (ch14):
  - **Low sound level** together with **staccato** was the strongest predictor of **fearful**.
  - **High sound level** together with **sharp timbre** was the strongest predictor of **angry**.
- **Tension studies** (Nielsen 1983/87, ch14): peaks of rated tension lined up mainly with fortissimo.

### Dynamic variation (ch14 §14.3.3; ch17)
- **Large swings** in loudness suggest **fear**. Small swings suggest happiness or activity (ch14).
- **Rapid loudness changes** suggest playfulness, pleading or **fear**. Few or no changes suggest **sadness, peace and dignity** (ch14).
- **Small dynamic range:** Watson 1942 linked it to dignity, sadness and peacefulness (ch14).
- **Dynamic variation matters more than tempo variation** for perceived expressiveness. In Kamenetsky et al. 1997, removing dynamic variation hurt expressiveness significantly, while removing tempo variation hurt it only marginally (ch14).
- **Felt emotion:** shaping dynamics may play a key role in actually moving listeners (Nagel et al. 2008; Timmers et al. 2006; ch17) [preliminary].

### Pitch level / register (ch14 §14.3.5, 14.2.3)
- **Pitch is ambiguous** [moderate-weak]. Unlike tempo and loudness, high and low pitch can each go with high or low activation (ch14 summary).
- **High pitch** goes with:
  - happy, graceful, serene, dreamy, exciting
  - surprise, potency, **anger, fear**, activity
- **Low pitch** goes with:
  - **sadness**, **dignity/solemnity**, vigour, excitement
  - boredom and pleasantness, depending on context
- **Octave transposition** (Rigg 1940a): shifting a phrase up an octave made it happier or less sorrowful, and shifting it down did the reverse. Transposing by a fifth or fourth had less effect, and by a step or semitone almost none (ch14).
- **Pitch and valence** (Ilie & Thompson 2006): higher pitch raised **tension arousal** in music, and lower pitch raised valence in music (ch14).
- **Register of harmonic intervals:**
  - Costa et al. 2000: two-note chords in a high register were rated more active (unstable, restless, tense) and stronger. Low-register two-note chords were rated more negatively (ch14).
  - Maher 1980: high intervals were rated happier and more powerful than low ones (ch14).

### Pitch variation and range (ch14 §14.3.5, 14.3.7)
- **Pitch variation:**
  - Large variation goes with happiness, pleasantness, activity and surprise.
  - **Small variation goes with disgust, anger, fear or boredom** (Scherer & Oshinsky 1977, ch14).
- **Melodic range:**
  - **Wide range** goes with joy, whimsy, **uneasiness and fear**.
  - **Narrow range** goes with sad, **dignified**, sentimental, tranquil, delicate and (oddly) triumphant (ch14).

### Melodic direction / contour (ch14 §14.3.7)
- **Ascending** melody goes with:
  - **dignity**, serenity, **tension**, happiness
  - fear, surprise, anger, potency
- **Descending** melody goes with exciting, graceful, vigorous, and **sadness**, especially when combined with minor mode (Gerardi & Gerken 1995). It also goes with boredom and pleasantness.
- **Direction is a weak cue overall** [weak as a main effect]. Hevner, Gundlach and Rigg found little or no effect, and Lindström 2006 found none overall. Contour does enter interactions, for example with rhythm.
- **Tension:** rising melody raised continuously rated tension (Nielsen) [moderate]. Melodic contour shaped tension in a Mozart sonata (Krumhansl 1996) (ch14).

### Intervals (ch14 §14.2.3, 14.3.6, 14.3.7)
- **Melodic intervals:**
  - Larger intervals sound more **powerful** (Maher & Berlyne 1982).
  - The **octave** sounds positive and strong.
  - The **minor second** sounds the most melancholic or sad.
- **Activity, dynamism and instability** (Costa et al. 2004) come from melodies with more **minor seconds, tritones, and leaps larger than an octave**.
- **Potency** (vigour, power) comes from more **unisons and octaves** (Costa et al. 2004).
- **Pleasantness** comes from clear tonality, more perfect fourths and minor sevenths, and few tritones (Costa et al. 2004).
- **Danger** (Smith & Williams 1999, South African children): the **major seventh and augmented fourth (tritone)** tended to evoke danger and violence. The perfect fourth suggested something desolate or old, and the octave something positive and strong [single study; culturally influenced].
- **Most displeasing harmonic intervals:** minor and major seconds, minor and major sevenths, and the minor ninth (Maher 1980).
- **Leaps versus steps:** leaps suggest excitement and stepwise motion suggests dullness. Peaceful melodies often move by step and then open into a leap (Thompson & Robitaille 1992).

### Harmony: consonance / dissonance / complexity (ch14 §14.3.8, 14.2.3)
- **Simple, consonant harmony** goes with happy, relaxed, graceful, serene, dreamy, **dignified, serious and majestic**.
- **Complex, dissonant harmony** goes with excitement, **tension**, vigour, **anger**, sadness and unpleasantness [moderate-strong].
- **Two-note chords:** dissonant ones were judged more negative, more active and *stronger* than consonant ones. Minor two-note chords sounded duller and weaker than major ones (Costa et al. 2000).
- **Tension:** dissonance and harmonic complexity raised continuously rated tension (Nielsen). Harmony and tonality also drove tension in Krumhansl 1996.
- **Little research on progressions:** the authors note there is almost nothing on how particular chord types or progressions affect expression (ch14 §14.4).

### Tonality / atonality / chromaticism (ch14 §14.3.9; ch13)
- **Composer study** (Thompson & Robitaille 1992):
  - Melodies written to sound joyful, dull or peaceful were tonal.
  - **Angry** melodies were rhythmically complex and used chromatic harmony or **atonality**.
  - **Sad** melodies were slow and implied minor or **chromatic** harmony.
- **Chromaticism and turmoil** (Simonton, ch13, following Meyer): composers often use chromatic notes to express feeling, with the unpredictable line mirroring inner turmoil. Blues "blue notes" are cited as a parallel.

### Rhythm (ch14 §14.3.10, 14.3.14)
- **Regular/smooth** rhythm goes with happiness, **dignity, majesty** and peace.
- **Irregular/rough** rhythm goes with amusement, **uneasiness** and **anger**. **Varied** rhythm goes with joy [moderate; terminology differs between authors].
- **Firm** rhythm (a chord on every beat) goes with sadness, **dignity** and vigour. **Flowing** rhythm (broken chords) goes with happy, graceful, dreamy and serene (Hevner).
- **Complexity:**
  - High melodic/harmonic/rhythmic complexity goes with tension or sadness. Low complexity goes with relaxation, joy or peace.
  - Rhythmic complexity raised rated tension (Nielsen).
- **Imberty 1979 on Debussy** [single study]. He combined formal complexity with a "dynamism" index built from density, loudness, accents and syncopation:
  - Low complexity with average dynamism gives positive emotion.
  - **High complexity with low dynamism gives melancholy and depression.**
  - **High complexity with high dynamism gives anxiety and aggressiveness.**
- **Accent placement** (Lindström 2006): putting the key notes of a mode (tonic, dominant, the third) on accented beats makes music feel more stable and strengthens the mode's emotion. Putting them off the accents lowers stability. Accent structure significantly predicted ratings.
- **Male versus female film themes** (Tagg 2006): themes heard as "male" (tough, cruel, hard, rebellious) had more repeated notes, more offbeats and syncopation, brass and percussion, and rock or jazz idioms. "Female" themes were slower, more legato, with longer phrases, static bass lines and no brass or percussion.

### Articulation (ch14 §14.3.11; ch17)
- **Staccato** goes with gaiety, energy, activity, **fear** and **anger**.
- **Legato** goes with sadness, **tenderness**, **solemnity** and softness [moderate].
- **Juslin 1997:**
  - Legato, slow tempo and soft spectrum best predicted **tender**.
  - Staccato and low level best predicted **fearful**.
  - Legato best predicted "expressive" (ch14).
- **Performance** (ch17):
  - Sadness uses legato with *little articulation variability*. Happiness uses staccato with *large articulation variability*.
  - Legato means a sounded-duration to inter-onset-interval ratio near 1.0. Staccato means a ratio below about 0.7 (Fig. 17.3 caption).
  - Mothers calm infants with slower, quieter speech and slowly falling pitch. They signal disapproval with brief, sharp, staccato-like contours (Papoušek 1996, ch17).

### Timbre / spectrum / instruments (ch14 §14.3.4; ch17; ch21)
- **Many high harmonics** (bright/sharp timbre) go with potency, **anger**, disgust, **fear**, activity and surprise: high activation.
- **Few or low harmonics** go with pleasantness, boredom, happiness or sadness.
- **Suppressed high harmonics** (soft/dark timbre) go with **tenderness** and sadness: lower activation (ch14).
- **Most decisive cue for anger:** sharp spectrum, together with loudness (Juslin 1997, ch14).
- **Performance code:** happiness uses bright timbre and sadness uses soft timbre (ch17).
- **Instruments:**
  - There is no systematic research on instrument timbre (ch14).
  - Behrens & Green 1993: sadness came across best on **voice or violin**, **anger on timpani**, and **fear on violin**.
  - Balkwill & Thompson 1999: in Hindustani music, Western listeners linked **strings with anger** and **flute with peace** (ch14).
- **Voice-like instruments:** Juslin speculates that violin and cello will be most effective at inducing emotion through contagion. They sound voice-like while going beyond what a voice can do (ch17) [hypothesis].
- **Spectral energy at 1–6 kHz** (ch21; Panksepp, Beeman): infant cries and adult screams carry much of their energy in the ear's most sensitive band, roughly 1–6 kHz. The operatic "singer's formant" (about 3 kHz) exploits the same band. Some listeners find opera "hysterical" because it sounds too close to screaming.

### Attack / amplitude envelope (ch14 §14.3.12; ch17)
- **Sharp envelope** (fast attack and decay) goes with **anger**, happiness, **surprise** and activity.
- **Round envelope** (slow attack) goes with **tenderness**, **sadness**, **fear**, disgust, boredom and potency (ch14).
- **Performance code:** sadness uses slow attacks and happiness fast attacks (ch17).

### Vibrato, intonation, timing variability (ch17)
- **Listed cues:** the cue chart includes vibrato, intonation, timing, tone decays and pauses. The prose does not give their emotion-specific values, which sit in the missing Fig. 17.2.
- **Means outweigh variability:** overall cue levels such as mean tempo explain more of listeners' judgements than cue variability does (Madison 2000). Still, patterns of timing and dynamic irregularity carry information that listeners can decode (Juslin & Madison 1999).
- **Note emphasis** (Lindström 1999): performers emphasise notes they consider emotionally significant using timing, dynamic and articulation accents. They emphasise "happy" notes in happy versions and de-emphasise them in sad ones.
- **Changing gestures for emphasis:** the way an important note is emphasised has changed over the decades. Sliding up to it, vibrating on it and swelling its amplitude have each been the fashion at different times (Leech-Wilkinson 2006a, ch17).
- **GERMS model, "S" (stylistic unexpectedness):** performers deliberately depart from stylistic conventions to add tension and unpredictability (ch17).

### Ornamentation (ch17, Timmers & Ashley 2007)
- **Anger** was played with the most complex ornaments.
- **Sadness** was played with the simplest ones: single appoggiaturas, often long and placed on the beat. Angry versions used few single appoggiaturas.

### Pauses / rests (ch14 §14.3.13, 14.2.2)
- **Rests after closure** (Margulis 2007): a rest after a resolved tonic feels less tense, and its tension drops faster. A rest after an unresolved predominant chord stays tense.
- **Pauses as tension builders:** pauses are among the formal devices that raised rated tension (Nielsen).

### Tempo / dynamic change over time (ch14 §14.4; ch21)
- **Little research on gradual change:** ch14 notes that crescendo, diminuendo, accelerando, ritardando, glissando, thickening texture and so on are almost unstudied. The authors propose treating them as "vitality affects" (Stern), the qualities of surging, fading, explosive or drawn-out motion.
- **Abrupt change and chills** (ch21): an **abrupt change of tempo or rhythm** and a **sudden large rise in loudness** are documented triggers of chills (see §4).

### Texture / number of voices (ch14; ch21)
- **Little direct research:** Webster & Weir 2005 found interactions between mode, texture and tempo (ch14).
- **Chills:** a new instrument or voice entering, or a sudden change of texture, triggers chills. So, less robustly, does widening the frequency range by adding low bass or high treble (ch21).

### Form / repetition / originality (ch14 §14.3.14; ch13)
- **Formal devices that raise tension:** repetition, condensing the material, sequential development, and pauses (Nielsen).
- **Large-scale structure:** tension peaks and then drops quickly at the end of big sections, and new ideas tend to arrive at low tension (Krumhansl 1996).
- **Global order matters little:** shuffling movements or sections barely changed listeners' ratings. Expression comes mostly from local structure, roughly six-second chunks (Konečni; Tillman & Bigand 1996).
- **Melodic originality** (ch13, Simonton): improbable note-to-note transitions correlate with perceived arousal potential (r = .21, a lower bound).

### Specific emotions/characters: quick lookup (all ch14 unless noted)
- **Fear/anxiety:**
  - Tempo: fast.
  - Level and dynamics: soft or low level, but with **large and rapid dynamic changes**.
  - Pitch: high pitch, wide melodic range, small pitch variation, ascending lines.
  - Articulation, timbre, attack: staccato, many high harmonics, round envelope.
  - Instrument: violin (Behrens & Green).
  - Imberty: anxiety comes from high complexity with high dynamism.
  - Juslin 1997: the best predictors were low level plus staccato. Fear was the hardest emotion to model (R² = .55).
- **Anger:**
  - Tempo and density: fast and dense.
  - Level and timbre: loud, sharp/bright, with sharp attack.
  - Rhythm and harmony: rough or irregular, complex, chromatic or atonal, dissonant.
  - Pitch: small pitch variation.
  - Articulation and instruments: staccato; timpani (Behrens & Green); strings (Balkwill & Thompson).
  - Ornaments: complex (ch17).
- **Sadness/grief:**
  - Tempo and level: slow, soft, with few dynamic changes.
  - Pitch: low pitch, narrow range, descending lines (especially in minor), minor seconds.
  - Mode and harmony: minor or chromatic.
  - Articulation and attack: legato with little variation, slow attacks.
  - Timbre and instruments: dark or soft; voice or violin.
  - Ornaments: simple, long, on-beat appoggiaturas (ch17).
  - Sadness and anger are the emotions communicated most accurately (ch17).
- **Tenderness:** slow, soft, legato, dark or soft spectrum, round or slow attack (Juslin 1997).
- **Solemnity/dignity:**
  - Tempo and level: slow, soft or few dynamic changes, small dynamic range.
  - Pitch and contour: low pitch, narrow range, ascending melody.
  - Rhythm: regular or firm.
  - Harmony and articulation: consonant, legato.
  - Mode: can be major ("solemn") or minor ("dignified").
- **Power/majesty/potency:**
  - Level and timbre: loud, many harmonics.
  - Intervals: large intervals, unisons and octaves.
  - Harmony and rhythm: majestic goes with simple consonant harmony and regular, smooth rhythm.
  - Register: high-register two-note chords rated stronger. Dissonant two-note chords also rated stronger.
- **Threat/menace/danger:** not named as such in the chapters.
  - The nearest findings are major sevenths and tritones linked to danger and violence (Smith & Williams) and the Phrygian mode linked to condemnation/punishment (Hill et al.).
  - Tagg's "tough/cruel/hard" themes used brass and percussion, repeated notes and syncopation.
  - Low-register two-note chords were rated more negative (Costa).
  - *Combining these into a "threat" profile is my inference.*
- **Tension:**
  - Loudness (fortissimo), ascending melody, higher pitch (tension arousal) and fast tempo.
  - Dense notes, dissonance, harmonic and rhythmic complexity.
  - Repetition, condensation, sequential development and pauses.
  - Unresolved chords before a rest.
  - Several kinds of expectation-based tension (§4).
- **Surprise:** fast tempo, high pitch, large pitch variation, ascending lines, sharp envelope, many high harmonics (Scherer & Oshinsky, ch14). For surprise built from expectation, see §4.
- **Uneasiness:** fast tempo, wide melodic range, irregular or rough rhythm.
- **Instability/"madness":**
  - Not discussed by name. Costa et al. found minor seconds, tritones and leaps beyond the octave convey *instability/dynamism*. High-register two-note chords sound unstable and restless.
  - Meyer (ch21): uncertainty, whether from missing expectations or from several conflicting ones, produces "apprehension and anxiety".
  - Prokofiev's deliberate "wrong notes" read as quirky, weird and impertinent (ch21).
  - Simonton (ch13): wartime works swing between very predictable and very unpredictable themes, which he reads as polarised emotion.
  - *Applying these to "madness" is my inference.*

## 2. How cues combine

- **Many factors per emotion** (ch14 §14.3.15): every structural factor affects many emotions, and every emotion depends on many factors. Single cues are rarely, if ever, decisive.
- **Additivity is the main story** (ch17):
  - Juslin & Timmers argue that most of the "strong interactions" in early studies were artefacts of not varying cues independently. A fast but sad-sounding piece does not prove an interaction: the fast tempo's happy pull may simply be outweighed by many sad cues.
  - Factorial studies (Juslin 1997b Exp. 2; Scherer & Oshinsky 1977) find some interactions, but **main effects explain most of the variance** (Juslin & Lindström 2009).
- **Interactions reported** (ch14). Most were found after the fact:
  - mode x tempo (Rigg 1940b)
  - loudness x pitch height and pitch height x tempo, for valence (Ilie & Thompson 2006)
  - scale direction x tempo, for happiness (Collier & Hubbard 2001b)
  - a three-way rhythm x contour x melodic progression interaction, for happiness (Lindström 2006)
  - two- and three-way mode x texture x tempo effects (Webster & Weir 2005)
- **The lens model** (ch17, Brunswik/Juslin):
  - Performers encode an emotion through several acoustic cues that are **probabilistic** (each only correlates with the emotion; fast tempo occurs in both happiness and anger) and **partly redundant** (a harder attack is both louder and brighter).
  - Listeners combine the same cues **additively**. No cue is necessary or sufficient, but the more cues agree, the more reliable the message.
  - **Vicarious functioning:** one cue can stand in for another, so different performers or instruments with different cue sets can communicate equally well.
  - **The price is low capacity:** redundancy limits how much information gets through, which is why basic emotions communicate more easily than complex ones. The system avoids gross errors (mistaking anger for joy) rather than achieving fine precision.
  - **Lens indices:**
    - Achievement (r_a): agreement between intended and perceived emotion.
    - Cue weights (β): how strongly each cue is used.
    - Matching (G): how similar performer and listener cue weights are.
    - Consistency (R_e for performers, R_s for listeners).
  - **Matching drives success:** for professionals, achievement depends mainly on matching (G).
- **Numbers:**
  - Linear regression models explain about **70–80%** of the variance in both performance and judgement (Juslin 1997b, 2000; ch17).
  - The expanded lens model, which adds composer cues to performer cues, explains about **75–85%** of listeners' emotion ratings from main effects alone. Composer x performer interactions add small but non-negligible predictive power (Juslin & Lindström 2003; ch17).
  - Unmodelled matching (shared residual) is small, so most of the variance is additive (Juslin & Madison 1999; ch17).
  - Juslin 1997 (ch14) manipulated tempo, level, spectrum, articulation and attack and explained R² = **.78–.88** for happy, sad, angry and tender, but only **.55 for fearful** and **.46 for "expressive"**. Fear depends on cues outside that set.
  - **Meta-analysis** (Juslin & Laukka 2003, ch17; 29 studies): decoding accuracy was about **70% correct with five choices**, close to facial and vocal expression. **Sadness and anger** were decoded best.
- **Relative weights / ranking of cues:**
  - Hevner: **tempo and mode** had the largest effects, then pitch level, harmony and rhythm. Melodic direction had little effect.
    - Sad/heavy depended on minor mode, then low pitch, then slow tempo, in that order.
    - Happy/bright depended on major mode, then fast tempo, then simple harmony (ch14).
  - Gundlach 1935: speed came first, then rhythm, interval distribution, orchestral range, loudness, mean pitch and melodic range (ch14).
  - Performance: tempo, sound level and timbre carry the most weight (ch17).
  - Summary in ch14:
    - Tempo, loudness and timbre give the clearest results; raising any of them raises activation.
    - **Tempo and loudness dominate most other factors.**
    - Activation is easier to judge than valence.
    - Pitch is more ambiguous.
  - Gagnon & Peretz 2003: tempo outweighed mode for happy versus sad (ch14).
  - Schellenberg et al. 2000: **pitch content mattered more than rhythm** for happy, sad and scary melodies. Rhythm, when it mattered, interacted with pitch (ch14).
- **Speed and robustness:**
  - Listeners recognise emotion from excerpts of **1 second or less** (Peretz et al. 1998; Bigand et al. 2005).
  - Gender and training make little difference (ch14).
  - Cue use is more consistent across pieces than across performers (ch17).
- **Complex/mixed emotions:** these are hard to communicate without context. Pride, for example, may be a blend of the happy and angry codes at lower activity (ch17).

## 3. Composer techniques (ch13, Simonton; with ch14/ch21 support)

Ch13 is a *historiometric* chapter. It content-analyses 15,618 themes by 479 composers and does not catalogue devices such as appoggiaturas or topics. It does give these actionable points:

- **Melodic originality as an arousal control.** Originality is measured as one minus the mean probability of the note-to-note transitions in a theme's first six notes.
  - **Common transitions:** in the repertoire, repeated dominant, repeated tonic and dominant-to-tonic together make up about 17% of transitions.
  - **Rare transitions:** chromatic notes are very rare.
  - **Examples:** Haydn's "Surprise" theme averages about .040 per transition. Liszt's *Faust Symphony* opening averages under .001.
  - **Effect on listeners:** more improbable transitions are perceived as more arousing, exciting or stimulating (Martindale & Uemura 1983, r = .21).
- **How composers build themes:** they open by establishing the key with tonic and dominant notes, then bring in the other scale degrees (ch13).
- **Inverted-U curve (Wundt curve):**
  - The most popular works have *moderate* melodic originality. The least popular have the *highest* originality, and very predictable works are middling.
  - Too much arousal potential produces stress, anxiety or even fear; Simonton cites the *Rite of Spring* premiere.
  - Listeners would rather be gently entertained than anxiously provoked.
  - Originality relates linearly to aesthetic significance (higher originality, more significance) and inversely to accessibility.
- **Where originality goes:**
  - Higher in instrumental than vocal music, and in chamber more than orchestral more than theatrical music. The more other resources a medium has (orchestration, staging), the less melody has to carry.
  - Higher in **outer movements** than inner ones. The composer grabs attention, relaxes in the middle, and raises intensity again for the finale.
  - Higher in **metrically complex** themes (3/2, 9/8) and in **minor**.
- **Trading resources:** when composers gain other ways to raise arousal (new forms, rhythms, orchestration), melodic originality falls. The larger the work, the weaker the link between originality and impact.
- **Habituation:** audiences get used to a given level of novelty, so arousal potential has to keep rising. This drives the historical rise in chromaticism, with peaks around Gesualdo/Monteverdi, late Romanticism (*Tristan*) and early Schoenberg.
- **Stress shows up in themes:**
  - Themes written during periods of biographical stress or (for Beethoven) poor health are more original or unpredictable.
  - Wartime works mix extremely predictable and extremely unpredictable themes. Simonton reads this as polarised emotion, swinging between resignation and despair, hope and fear (Shostakovich's *Leningrad*).
- **"Swan-song" effect:** works written close to the composer's death have *simpler, more predictable* melodies and are shorter, yet more popular and more highly rated. Simonton reads them as resignation or inner peace, communicating the most with the least (Mozart *Ave Verum*, Strauss *Four Last Songs*).
- **Chromaticism:** chromatic notes express feeling, their unpredictability mirroring inner turmoil (Meyer, cited in ch13).
- **Expectation devices** (ch21 and ch17, citing Meyer):
  - The **deceptive cadence** (V–vi in place of V–I) denies an urgent expected resolution.
  - **Delaying the return of a theme** in a rondo builds tension.
  - **Performers** can heighten violations already in the score by emphasising key notes, or by playing against performance conventions.
- **Composer-written emotional melodies** (Thompson & Robitaille 1992, ch14). Listeners generally recognised the intended emotions.
  - **Joy:** strongly tonal, varied rhythm.
  - **Sorrow:** slow, implied minor or chromatic harmony.
  - **Excitement:** fast, with leaps and high pitches.
  - **Dullness:** tonal, stepwise.
  - **Anger:** rhythmically complex, chromatic or atonal.
  - **Peace:** tonal and slow, stepwise motion opening into leaps.
- **Cooke's "basic terms":** an ascending major 1–3–5 suggests outgoing joy, and its minor form suggests an assertion of sorrow. These received only weak empirical support. Gabriel 1978 found mostly negative results, and Kaminska & Woolf 2000 supported Cooke only for joy and sorrow (ch14).

## 4. Thrills / chills / tension / surprise (ch21, Huron & Margulis)

- **Who gets chills (frisson):**
  - Only about half of people report music-induced chills: 47% of Goldstein's non-musician sample never had them, 55% of Grewe's participants did, and Konečni induced them in 35%. About 90% of music students report them.
  - They are more common in women, in less sensation-seeking people, and in listeners who know classical music and listen attentively.
  - Familiarity does *not* cause chills: Guhn et al. found a zero correlation.
- **Agreed triggers.** Among researchers there is considerable agreement on these correlates:
  1. **A rapid, large change in loudness, especially a sudden forte (subito forte).** This is the most important acoustic correlate.
  2. **Widening the frequency range** by adding low bass and/or high treble (less robust).
  3. **One or more instruments or voices entering.**
  4. **The return of a melody, theme or motive.**
  5. **An abrupt change of tempo or rhythm.**
  6. **A new or unprepared harmony.**
  7. **An abrupt modulation.**
  8. **A sudden change of texture.**
  9. **"Sad" music** (slow, quiet, minor) is about **twice as likely** to cause chills as happy music (Panksepp 1995).
- **What the triggers share:**
  - **Unexpectedness:** they are abrupt, sudden, new or unprepared.
  - **High energy:** more loudness or more sound sources.
  - **Contrast:** slow, quiet passages make a strong background for sudden energetic events.
- **Worked examples:**
  - **Pink Floyd, "The Final Cut":** a phrase sung almost in a whisper is followed by one sung as a shout.
  - **Schoenberg, *Verklärte Nacht* bars 225–30.** Three surprises land together:
    - A subito forte.
    - An unexpected D-major chord. Nothing has prepared G minor, and the preceding E-flat chord is minor rather than major.
    - A downbeat that is no longer expected, because the earlier chords kept avoiding the downbeat and wore down the sense of metre.
- **Two theories of why chills happen:**
  - **Panksepp:** chill-inducing passages resemble separation or distress calls. Energy around 1–6 kHz, as in cries, screams and the operatic singer's formant, grabs attention.
  - **Huron (contrastive valence):** a fast, worst-case alarm reaction is triggered by loud, scream-like or surprising sounds. About half a second later, a slower appraisal concludes "it's just music". The swing from negative to neutral or positive is felt as pleasure.
    - The same mechanism can produce **laughter** or **awe** instead.
    - These defensive reactions resist habituation, so a passage can keep giving chills on repeat hearings.
- **Kinds of expectation and their effects:**
  - **Meyer:** emotion comes from an expectation being thwarted. **Uncertainty** produces "apprehension and anxiety", whether it comes from having no clear expectation (an unfamiliar style) or from several competing ones (ambiguity).
  - **Typical pieces:** music where everything is expected and fulfilled is emotionally neutral and rare. Most works mix fulfilled and thwarted expectations.
  - **Margulis's three tensions:**
    - **Surprise-tension:** an unpredictable event. It feels intense and dynamic and draws attention.
    - **Denial-tension:** a surprise in place of a strongly expected event. It feels like intention or will in the music.
    - **Expectancy-tension:** an event that strongly implies a particular next event. It feels forward-driving.
  - **Huron's ITPRA:**
    - Before the event: imagination, then rising tension or arousal as it approaches.
    - After the event: a prediction response (pleasure if predicted, unease if not), a fast defensive reaction, then a slower appraisal.
    - The **prediction effect:** listeners like familiar music because it is predictable. For example, ending on the tonic satisfies more than ending on the leading tone.
  - **Narmour:**
    - Similarity implies more similarity: small steps in one direction imply more of the same.
    - Difference implies more difference: a big leap followed by a step back creates closure.
    - Possible responses are "exhilaration" when an inference is confirmed, "irony" when it is partly right, and "shock" when it is wrong.
  - **Gap fill / post-skip reversal:** trained Western listeners *expect* a large leap to be followed by a change of direction. That makes it a usable expectation even though, statistically, it is just regression to the mean.
- **Schematic versus veridical expectations** (Bharucha):
  - A deceptive cadence still surprises at the level of style knowledge even after the listener knows the piece, so repetition does not fully dull it.
  - Surprise can also run the other way, as in Schickele's banal version of a Beethoven theme, which works as humour.
- **Prokofiev's "wrong notes"** (*Peter and the Wolf*, bar 3):
  - A sudden flat-submediant harmony arrives with a melody swinging between E-flat and B-natural, an augmented fifth that clashes with the accompaniment.
  - The probability of scale degree 7 moving to flat 3 is about .00004. Typical major-key transitions carry 3–5 bits of information.
  - **Recipe:** displace a note by a semitone, usually flat; approach and leave it by leap; repeat it straight away so it sounds intended.
  - **Effect:** quirky, weird, impertinent. It works best against a conventional, neoclassical background.
- **Physiology:**
  - Chills go with spikes in skin conductance and correlate with heart rate.
  - Blood & Zatorre found reward areas such as the nucleus accumbens light up during chills, and amygdala activity drops.
  - Unexpected harmonies change skin conductance and heart rate (Steinbeis et al. 2006).

## 5. Film music (ch31, Cohen)

- **Cohen's eight functions** (1999):
  1. masking noise
  2. continuity across cuts
  3. directing attention, through structural or associative congruence
  4. inducing mood, as in opening credits
  5. conveying meaning and advancing the story, *especially in ambiguous situations*
  6. memory and association, including **leitmotif**
  7. heightening absorption and arousal
  8. adding aesthetic value
- **Leitmotif:** pairing a theme repeatedly with a character or event until the theme alone calls it up, even when the character is absent. Wagner is usually credited as the first to exploit it, and Saint-Saëns attributed its power to psychological principles. Music can also cue mood-dependent memories (Eich). Ch31 reports no experiment on leitmotif itself.
- **Music takes an object:** in multimedia, the emotional associations of music attach automatically to whatever is the visual focus of attention or the topic of the story (Cook 1998). In ads, a car takes on the vitality and prestige of the classical music behind it.
- **Characterisation studies:**
  - **Marshall & Cohen 1988 (Heider–Simmel animated shapes):** two soundtracks of similar overall activity changed *which* character seemed most active. Under one, the big triangle seemed most active; under the other, the small one did. The explanation: shared accent patterns between music and movement pull attention to that figure, and the music's associations then stick to it. This is the basis of the Congruence-Associationist Model (CAM).
  - **Tan, Spackman & Bezdek 2007:** characters with neutral faces were read as happy, sad, fearful or angry according to the music, whether the music came *before or after* the character appeared. Viewers credited the music *and the (neutral) face* for the emotion they saw.
  - **Shevy 2007:** for a good character in a bad setting, happy versus ominous rock music shifted how the *setting* was read. Either kind of music made judgements of character and setting more alike.
  - **Bullerjahn & Güldenring 1994:**
    - Five professional scores (crime, melodrama and others) for one ten-minute scene changed the perceived emotion, the genre (horror, comedy, thriller, crime), the reasons behind the protagonist's actions, and how viewers expected the film to end.
    - A final major chord made viewers assume a reconciliation after an argument.
  - **Tan, Spackman & Wakefield 2008 (*Minority Report*):**
    - The same "Moon River" recording heard as diegetic (inside the story) versus non-diegetic score changed how the scene and characters were read.
      - Diegetic: the scene was rated calmest.
      - As score: the scene was rated least calm and the relationship most tense/antagonistic.
      - The chapter's wording on the tense dimension is internally inconsistent; it also attributes "highest tense/antagonistic" to the diegetic version.
    - The male character seemed more fearful and excited and less romantically interested with the diegetic version.
    - Mood-incongruent music has distinct effects of its own.
- **Closure** (Thompson, Russo & Sinclair 1994):
  - Ending a soundtrack on a V–I cadence made a film clip feel more finished than an ending off the tonic.
  - Visual closure weighed slightly more than musical closure.
  - Viewers credited their judgements almost entirely to the visuals, so the music's role was almost wholly implicit.
- **Congruence, memory and foreshadowing** (Boltz et al. 1991; Boltz 2004):
  - **Music during an outcome:** if its mood matches the scene, the scene is remembered better. A mismatched mood drops recall to the no-music level.
  - **Music before an outcome (foreshadowing):** the effect reverses. Foreshadowing that *mismatches* the outcome makes the episode more memorable, because a violated expectation sticks.
  - **Joint encoding:** congruent mood leads to integrated audio-visual memory. Incongruence splits attention onto one channel.
- **Absorption** (Cohen, MacMillan & Drew 2006):
  - In *Witness*, the music-only soundtrack raised absorption. Jarre's music *replaces* the realistic sound at the boy's moment of recognition, and audiences rarely notice the departure from reality.
  - With weaker music (the *Day of the Jackal* clip), there was no gain.
- **Music works below awareness:**
  - Audiences take the emotional meaning but ignore the sound itself, a kind of "inattentional deafness" or "unheard melodies" (Gorbman).
  - Precise synchronisation of music and action ("Mickey Mousing") hides the source of the music and makes it inaudible (Kalinak).
  - When there is no dialogue or sound effect, music draws attention to itself (Kassabian).
  - Desynchronising music and picture by 500 ms reduced how well they seemed to match (Iwamiya 1994).
- **Genuine emotion** (Tan's criteria, applied by Cohen): film music meets them.
  - It takes priority in steering emotion.
  - It finds an object.
  - It has stimulus-specific meaning.
  - It adds to apparent reality (Steiner's *King Kong*).
  - It responds to change through expectation.
  - It runs its course outside conscious control, carried along by tension and resolution.
- **Composer's craft:**
  - The film composer has to know how shared accents focus attention, how to stay below awareness, how to set mood, and how associations prompt inferences, whether by reinforcement or counterpoint.
  - Practical constraints:
    - hit cues to a fraction of a second
    - match or create the mood
    - be unheard yet memorable
    - never drown out dialogue
  - Lipscomb & Kendall 1994: viewers most often picked the composer's actual cue for a *Star Trek IV* scene as the best fit.
- **Emotional polyphony:** music can carry several emotions at once. Korngold's *Sea Hawk* love music, for instance, blends lyric love (arching, repeated rising sevenths) with a heroic horn call of adventure.
- **Gender of themes** (Tagg 2006, in ch14): film themes heard as tough, cruel or hard used brass and percussion, repeated notes, syncopation and offbeats, and rock or jazz idioms.
- **Not covered:** villain-specific scoring, menace and character-type themes are not addressed empirically in ch31. The nearest findings are the "ominous" music in Shevy 2007, fear and anger music recolouring neutral faces (Tan et al. 2007), and mood-incongruent foreshadowing (Boltz).

## 6. Actionable rules

Each rule notes its source chapter or chapters. *(Inference)* marks rules that combine findings in ways the text itself does not.

1. **Set arousal with tempo and loudness first.** These two cues outweigh most others, and arousal is easier to read than valence. Use a separate cue for valence. (ch14, ch17)
2. **Set valence with mode.** Major tilts positive, minor negative. A fast minor theme can still sound playful, so other cues have to agree for a clear minor-sad reading. (ch14)
3. **Stack redundant cues; never rely on one.** Each cue is probabilistic and they add up, so the more cues point the same way, the more reliably the emotion reads. A missing cue can be covered by another. (ch17)
4. **Sadness/grief:**
   - slow tempo, soft dynamics with little dynamic change
   - low register, narrow range, descending minor lines with minor seconds, chromatic harmony
   - legato with uniform note lengths, slow attacks, dark timbre
   - solo voice or violin
   - for ornaments, long single appoggiaturas on the beat

   (ch14, ch17)
5. **Tenderness:** slow, quiet, legato, soft or dark timbre (cut the high partials), gentle attacks. (ch14, Juslin 1997)
6. **Anger:**
   - loud, with bright or harsh timbre (strongest predictors)
   - fast and dense
   - sharp attacks, staccato
   - rough or irregular, complex rhythm; chromatic, dissonant or atonal harmony; narrow pitch variation
   - timpani or aggressive strings
   - for ornaments, busy complex figures

   (ch14, ch17)
7. **Fear/anxiety:**
   - mostly quiet with staccato (strongest predictors)
   - large, rapid dynamic swings
   - fast tempo, wide melodic range, high register, restricted pitch variation, rising lines
   - violin

   Fear is the least predictable emotion from simple cues (R² = .55), so add expectation-based uncertainty (rule 21). (ch14, ch21)
8. **Uneasiness:** fast tempo, wide melodic range, irregular or rough rhythm. (ch14)
9. **Solemnity/dignity:**
   - slow, with little dynamic change and a small dynamic range
   - low register, narrow range
   - regular or firm rhythm (a chord on each beat)
   - simple consonant harmony, legato
   - ascending lines are acceptable

   (ch14)
10. **Power/majesty/potency:**
    - loud, with rich and bright harmonics
    - large melodic intervals, unisons and octaves
    - consonant harmony with regular, smooth rhythm for "majestic"

    (ch14)
11. **Tension that builds:**
    - get louder (tension peaks line up with fortissimo)
    - raise the line
    - add notes per beat
    - add dissonance and harmonic or rhythmic complexity
    - repeat, condense and sequence the material
    - insert pauses

    (ch14)
12. **Release tension with closure.** End sections on the tonic and let tension drop quickly after a peak. A rest right after an unresolved predominant chord *keeps* tension hanging; a rest after the tonic releases it. (ch14)
13. **Instability/restlessness:**
    - write melodies rich in minor seconds, tritones and leaps beyond the octave
    - voice dissonant dyads high (rated restless, tense and strong)

    (ch14, Costa et al.)
14. **Danger/violence:** use the major seventh and tritone (augmented fourth) as melodic intervals. Single study, culturally shaped. (ch14, Smith & Williams)
15. **Condemnation/punishment, or a dark, ominous religious colour:** use the Phrygian mode rather than major/Ionian. Single study. (ch14, Hill et al.)
16. **Threat** *(inference)*:
    - low register (rated more negative)
    - minor or Phrygian, dissonance with tritones and sevenths
    - loud brass and percussion with repeated notes and syncopation
    - sudden dynamic accents

    Combined from ch14 (Costa; Smith & Williams; Hill; Tagg) and ch21.
17. **"Madness"/derangement** *(inference)*:
    - high melodic complexity with high dynamism (Imberty: anxiety and aggression)
    - irregular rhythm
    - "wrong notes" shifted by a semitone and repeated so they sound deliberate
    - ambiguous harmony that sets up several conflicting expectations

    Combined from ch14 and ch21.
18. **Engineer chills:**
    - set up a quiet, slow, often minor passage
    - then hit a sudden forte, ideally together with a new voice or instrument, an unprepared chord or abrupt modulation, a texture change, a widened range (low bass plus high treble), or the return of the main theme

    Stack several surprises on one downbeat, as in the *Verklärte Nacht* example. (ch21)
19. **A whisper-to-shout contrast** is a proven chill device in vocal lines. (ch21, Pink Floyd example)
20. **Delay the expected.** Withhold a theme's expected return, or replace V–I with V–vi. Both build tension and emotion, and they still work on listeners who know the piece. (ch21, Meyer/Bharucha)
21. **Create anxious uncertainty** by withholding clear expectations (unfamiliar idiom) or by setting up several competing ones (ambiguous harmony or metre). Meyer links this to apprehension and anxiety. (ch21)
22. **Wear down the metre before a big moment.** Keep chords off the downbeat so the eventual downbeat arrival is itself a surprise. (ch21)
23. **Quirky or impertinent characters** (tricksters, odd NPCs): over a conventional, tonal background, displace melody notes by a semitone (usually flat), approach and leave them by leap, and repeat at once. (ch21, Prokofiev)
24. **Humour by banality:** once listeners know a theme, swapping its adventurous continuation for a cliché can be funny. (ch21, Schickele)
25. **Ration surprise.** Arousal potential follows an inverted U: moderate unpredictability is most liked, and excessive novelty causes stress or anxiety. Keep themes moderately original; save extreme improbability for deliberate unease. (ch13)
26. **Put the most original material in outer sections** (openings and finales) and relax predictability in the middle. (ch13)
27. **Use richer orchestration or form to buy simpler melodies.** When orchestration, rhythm or staging carry the arousal, melodies can be more predictable. (ch13)
28. **Peace, resignation or a "farewell":** simplify the melody (predictable transitions, short forms). (ch13, swan-song)
29. **Emotional turmoil or wartime:** alternate very predictable with very unpredictable themes and add chromaticism. (ch13)
30. **Lock music accents to on-screen or narrated action** to direct attention. The music's emotion then attaches to whatever it is synchronised with, which can be the hero, the villain or a prop. Keep sync tight; a 500 ms offset already hurts. (ch31, Marshall & Cohen; CAM; Iwamiya)
31. **Recolour a neutral character or scene with music.** Fear, anger, happy or sad cues placed just before *or* just after a neutral character appears shift how the audience reads that character's emotion. (ch31, Tan et al. 2007)
32. **Leitmotifs:** pair a theme consistently with a character or event so it can later summon them in their absence (foreshadowing, memory). (ch31)
33. **Foreshadow with mismatched mood to make a twist memorable.** During the outcome itself, match the mood to strengthen memory of the scene. (ch31, Boltz)
34. **Signal resolution or reconciliation** with a full V–I cadence or final major chord. Leave the ending off the tonic for open, unresolved scenes. Listeners will credit the visuals or narration, not the music. (ch31, Thompson et al.; Bullerjahn & Güldenring)
35. **Diegetic versus non-diegetic matters.** The same tune played "in-world" (a tavern bard) versus as underscore changes how scene and characters are read. Pleasant in-world music can sit against tension, while the same piece as score reads differently. (ch31, Tan et al. 2008)
36. **At the key revelation, consider replacing ambient sound with music** to maximise absorption, as in *Witness*. Keep music out of the way of dialogue. (ch31)
37. **Tough, hard or cruel characters versus gentle ones:**
    - tough: brass and percussion, repeated notes, syncopation and offbeats
    - gentle: slower, legato, long phrases, static bass, no brass or percussion

    (ch14, Tagg)
38. **Peace/serenity:** slow, tonal, stepwise motion opening into a leap, flute timbre, low melodic and rhythmic complexity, regular flowing rhythm. (ch14)
39. **Shape dynamics within phrases.** Dynamic variation adds more expressiveness than tempo variation (rubato), and emphasising significant notes with accents of time, loudness or articulation intensifies the target emotion. (ch14, ch17)
40. **Use voice-like solo lines (violin, cello, voice) for emotional contagion.** The closer to a voice, the more contagious, according to Juslin's hypothesis. Bright high-frequency energy around 1–6 kHz (screams, the singer's formant) is maximally attention-grabbing. (ch17, ch21)
